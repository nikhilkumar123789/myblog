## Why mathematics keeps finding its way into my life

I don't think of mathematics as something that belongs only in classrooms, textbooks, or problem sets anymore.

For me, it has gradually become a way of looking at things.

There is something deeply satisfying about taking a complicated problem and asking:

> **What is actually going on here?**

What can be simplified? What pattern is hiding underneath the noise? Which assumptions matter, and which ones don't?

That habit of thinking has followed me far beyond mathematics itself.

I see it in markets, where thousands of seemingly unrelated movements can sometimes be traced back to a handful of forces. I see it in programming, where an elegant abstraction can turn an ugly problem into a manageable one. I see it in statistics, where uncertainty stops being something to fear and becomes something that can be described.

And, increasingly, I see it in ordinary life.

## The appeal of difficult problems

I have always been drawn to problems that don't immediately give up their answer.

There is a particular kind of satisfaction in sitting with something confusing for a while. At first, everything looks disconnected. Then one small observation changes the picture. A relationship appears. A better question emerges. Eventually, what looked impossibly complicated becomes understandable.

The answer is almost secondary.

What matters is learning how to think your way toward it.

That is probably why I keep returning to mathematics. It rewards patience. It teaches precision without demanding that everything become rigid. And it has a wonderful way of reminding me that being stuck is often just another stage of understanding.

## From formulas to intuition

The older I get, the less interested I am in mathematics as a collection of formulas.

I care much more about intuition.

A probability distribution is not just an equation. It is a way of describing uncertainty.

A derivative is not just notation. It tells us how something changes.

A stochastic process is not just a mathematical object. It is a language for thinking about systems whose future cannot be known exactly.

A model is not reality.

It is a useful approximation of reality — and knowing where that approximation breaks down is often more important than knowing how to build it.

That distinction has become increasingly important to me in my work. The mathematics is powerful, but the real skill is knowing what question the mathematics is actually answering.

## Why I want to write about it

This blog is partly an attempt to document that journey.

I want to write about mathematics, statistics, probability, quantitative finance, technology, and the ideas that connect them. But I don't want this to become another collection of technical notes that exist only to demonstrate that I know a formula.

I want to write about **why things work**.

Sometimes that might mean taking a mathematical concept and explaining it visually.

Sometimes it might mean exploring an idea from quantitative finance.

Sometimes it might be a problem that made me think differently.

And sometimes it might simply be an observation about how mathematical thinking changes the way we see the world.

## A personal philosophy

I am slowly becoming convinced that a good life has something in common with a good mathematical problem.

It should be difficult enough to keep you curious.

It should have room for mistakes.

It should occasionally surprise you.

And you should be able to look back after some time and realise that the things that once seemed impossible were simply things you had not learned how to approach yet.

I don't want to optimise every part of my life.

I want to remain curious about it.

There is a difference.

Optimisation asks:

> How can I make this better?

Curiosity asks:

> What happens if I look at this differently?

I think the second question is often where the interesting things begin.

## What comes next

This blog will be a place for those questions.

There will be mathematics, but also markets, models, code, ideas, mistakes, experiments, and the occasional rabbit hole that starts with a simple question and ends somewhere completely unexpected.

I don't expect to have everything figured out.

I'd rather have better questions.

And perhaps that is the most mathematical thing of all.
